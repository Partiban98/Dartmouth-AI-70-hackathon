- The theme is one question: **what must remain human?** The video must open with the sentence "In our project, a person always ___."
- Three judge questions, said twice: **Who is this for? What could go wrong? Does it help people or decide for them?**
- "We're looking for more of the idea, the why behind it, versus the plumbing and infrastructure of your app. The idea is really what matters."
- "Call out why it matters, what are some of the risks or consequences, and what remains human throughout."
- Think about the negatives too: power concentration, autonomy, trust.

- Judges: Anthropic staff plus faculty. Prizes: 1st Claude Max for half a year + $750 + 30 min with an Anthropic expert.

## The one-line answer we already have

**"In our project, a person always assigns the grade."** The receipt on screen already says "Assigned by you (TA)". Open with the sentence, close with the receipt. Everything between is proof.

## Why this beats the room

Most teams in that room are building "AI that does X for you" (the team next to us pitched the same adaptive-learning platform we started with; that is why we pivoted to grading). GuruAI is the opposite: **AI that cannot do the human part even if you ask it to.** The model never sees a point value, never computes, never writes a grade; the grade is a text box a named person fills in; the log records who. Our answer to "who answers for Claude" is: a person, by name, every time, and here is the log.

That is not a feature claim, it is a design constraint, and we can show it in 10 seconds: the model's JSON has three fields, code, quote, confidence. Nothing else counts.

## The second thing nobody else will have: a marking scheme that carries precedents, owned by the instructor

Quiz 3 Q5 in the DEMO-STATS 201 set has six accepted ways to solve it and a rubric that started with one. The precedents a grading team agrees on ("a p-value range containing the exact value earns full credit", "a different standard error is a variant, no deduction", "a wrong decision that follows from the student's own wrong number is not a second error") are a versioned, visible table in the app, validated by code, and graders can propose new ones from a single decision. That is the answer to **power concentration**: the rules are not in one grader's head or one model's weights; they are on screen, versioned, and owned by the instructor. Say that sentence in the video.

Every paper and name in both sets is synthetic. The live loop in the video runs on DEMO-STATS 101. The Start screen now opens on three doors; for the video click "or try the sample papers" to reach the same Question step, and optionally add one line over the code card: "Each TA joins with the head TA's code. The scheme is shared; the names never leave their laptop."
